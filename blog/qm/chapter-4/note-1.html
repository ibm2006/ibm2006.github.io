4.1. The Schrodinger Equation

Chapter 4 / Note 1

The Hamiltonian operator in three dimensions is written as follows:

$$\hat H=\frac{1}{2}mv^2+V=\frac{1}{2m}(p_x^2+p_y^2+p_z^2)+V$$

Here, $p_x=-i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial x}$, and it is clear that the same kind of expression holds for $y$ and $z$. Therefore, the Schrodinger equation is simply written as

$$i\hbar \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla ^2 \Psi+V\Psi$$

Of course, the time-independent Schrodinger equation is $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 \psi+V\psi=E\psi$$ and for stationary states $\psi_n$ satisfying this equation, the general solution of the Schrodinger equation is expressed as a linear combination of $$\Psi_n(r,t)=\psi_n(r)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}.$$

4.1.1. Spherical Coordinates

In this section, we discuss the Schrodinger equation in spherical coordinates when the potential $V$ has spherical symmetry.

Recall that the Laplacian in spherical coordinates is

$$\nabla^2=\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r})+\frac{1}{r^2\sin \theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}(\sin \theta\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta})+\frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \phi^2}).$$

Substitute this into the Schrodinger equation and simplify. First, consider separation of variables of the form $\psi(r,\theta,\phi)=R(r)Y(\theta,\phi)$. Substituting this, the whole expression eventually becomes

$$(\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2\frac{dR}{dr})-\frac{2mr^2}{\hbar^2}(V(r)-E))+\frac{1}{Y}(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta})+\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial \phi^2})=0.$$

Clearly, the first term is an expression in $r$, and the second term is an expression in $\theta,\phi$, so both must be constants. For some reason, it becomes convenient to set $$\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2\frac{dR}{dr})-\frac{2mr^2}{\hbar^2}(V(r)-E)=l(l+1).$$ Then $$\frac{1}{Y}(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta})+\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial \phi^2})=-l(l+1).$$

4.1.2. The Angular Equation

First, analyze the equation involving the two angles $\theta,\phi$. It can be described as follows:

$$\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial \theta})+\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial \phi^2}=-l(l+1)\sin^2 \theta Y.$$

This equation can again be analyzed by applying separation of variables to $Y$. Put $$Y(\theta,\phi)=\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\phi).$$ After simplifying the expression, we obtain $$(\frac{1}{\Phi}(\sin\theta\frac{d}{d\theta}(\sin\theta \frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}))+l(l+1)\sin^2\theta)+\frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\phi^2}=0.$$ Again, each term depends on a different variable, so each must be a constant. This time, set that constant to be $m^2$.

$$\frac{1}{\Phi}(\sin\theta\frac{d}{d\theta}(\sin\theta \frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}))+l(l+1)\sin^2\theta=m^2,\quad \frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\phi^2}=-m^2.$$

The second equation is obvious, and solving it gives $\Phi(\phi)=e^{im\phi}$. Since $\phi$ is an angle, we need $\Phi(\phi)=\Phi(\phi+2\pi)$, and for this to hold, $m$ must be an integer.

Now analyze the first equation. In fact, solving this kind of equation is quite difficult, and facts about this form of equation are already known. The solution is $$\Theta(\theta)=AP_l^m(\cos\theta),$$ where $P_l^m$ is the associated Legendre function, defined by $$P_l^m(x)\equiv (-1)^m(1-x^2)^{m/2}(\frac{d}{dx})^m P_l(x).$$ Here, $P_l$ is the Legendre polynomial, defined by $$P_l(x)\equiv \frac{1}{2^l l!}(\frac{d}{dx})^l (x^2-1)^l.$$

In general, memorizing these formulas or tables of polynomials is fairly inefficient, so it is better to know only a few important properties.

After very difficult calculations, one can obtain the normalized form of the $Y$ functions, called spherical harmonics.

$$Y_l^m(\theta,\phi)=\sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}e^{im\phi}P_l^m(\cos\theta).$$

4.1.3. The Radial Equation

The remaining differential equation to solve is

$$\frac{d}{dr}(r^2\frac{dR}{dr})-\frac{2mr^2}{\hbar^2}(V(r)-E)R=l(l+1)R.$$

Define $u(r)\equiv rR(r)$. Then, after sufficient calculation, this can be reduced to

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2}+(V+\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2})u=Eu.$$

This can be interpreted as the one-dimensional Schrodinger equation for $V_{eff}=V+\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}$. The additional term $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(l(l+1)/r^2)$ plays a role corresponding to "centrifugal force" in classical mechanics.
