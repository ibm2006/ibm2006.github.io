My APIO Problems

Based on the attached PDF note

APIO 2026 was held recently, and I found out that a problem I submitted through the Call for Tasks was used. This means that my third APIO problem, and my third consecutive APIO problem from 2024 through 2026, has now been made public. To celebrate three consecutive APIO tasks, I decided to write some brief thoughts and behind-the-scenes notes about the three problems I have authored so far.

APIO 2024: Task C, Magic Show

https://qoj.ac/contest/1684/problem/8726

To be honest, this problem may be the biggest mistake in my history as a problemsetter.

The contest this problem was originally meant for was "Anime Cup 2." This was not the Anime Cup 2 hosted by hyperbolic, but rather a contest that had been semi-prepared in the same server after Anime Cup 1, hosted long ago by sait2000, and then abandoned.

The solution I first had in mind was extremely complicated, but it also contained many elegant and impressive ideas. When APIO 2024 Call for Tasks was running, I was thinking about what problem to submit. Since Anime Cup 2 showed no sign of progress, I thought it would be fine to submit this problem. I submitted it with the mindset that I had nothing to lose: if there were a much simpler unintended solution than the official one, the problem would be rejected, and learning about that existence would still be a worthwhile result.

Surprisingly, however, the APIO 2024 Committee selected this problem. That made me believe that my complicated solution was actually a very efficient one, because if a sufficiently easy and simple solution existed, I thought the committee would have found it.

I provided the basic setup for the problem as instructed by the committee. For two weeks until the contest day, there was no contact at all, so I even considered the possibility that I had submitted the problem to some scam organization pretending to be the APIO committee. Looking back, there may not have been much reason for the author and committee to communicate frequently, but at the time this was enough to create that suspicion.

I remember that the contest day was the day of my high school alumni gathering. While I was there, I contacted qwerasdfzxcl, who had taken the contest, to check whether my problem had actually appeared. qwerasdfzxcl said they had solved every problem except mine, and at that moment I had no choice but to believe that my solution was essentially optimal.

After the contest, however, I saw in the Codeforces discussion thread that there was an extremely simple and efficient solution using CRT, and I was deeply shocked. This is only my inference, but there was also a story that China had 56 perfect scorers because my problem, which had been expected to be the hardest, was actually very easy.

For this reason, setting this problem, or failing to find the simple CRT solution, became the biggest embarrassing episode in my problemsetting history. Even now, my friends, including qwerasdfzxcl, continue to make various memes based on this event. It was certainly my mistake, but I do still think that if there was a real issue with the problem, it would have been nice if the committee had found it.

What I learned from this experience is that making a two-step problem about labeled trees is not a particularly good idea.

APIO 2025: Task C, Rotating Lines

https://qoj.ac/contest/2031/problem/9207

I submitted this problem without really expecting it to be selected. The basic idea came from the following problem shown to me by a friend:

Given $2n$ lines passing through the origin, consider the total sum of the angles between every pair of lines. What is the maximum possible value of this sum?

After thinking about it for a while, I observed that there was a simple solution using graph modeling. Treat each line as a vertex, and connect two vertices exactly when the angle between the two lines is $0$ or $\pi/2$. The graph constructed in this way has some number of components.

Now consider rotating all lines in a particular component by $d\theta$. The total sum of angles changes as a linear function of $d\theta$. Therefore, by rotating the component in one direction, the total sum of angles increases until an edge is first added between this component and another component. Repeating this eventually reaches a point where the whole graph becomes connected.

When all lines are parallel or perpendicular, it is clear that it is optimal to split the lines into two groups of $n$ lines each and add the angles.

Of course, easier solutions and various other solutions exist, but I thought the advantage of this solution was that for any given configuration of lines, a certain operation can increment the total angle and reach the maximum. I wrote and submitted the problem based on this point.

I did not think it was a particularly beautiful problem and did not expect much, so I was somewhat surprised to receive the email saying it had been selected.

This problem also had some issues. The original version I submitted required difficult optimization with efficient data structures for large $n$. This time, I was able to communicate with a committee member and adjust details of the problem. One concern raised was that the implementation was somewhat complicated compared with the difficulty of the idea, so I replied that making $n$ smaller would also be fine.

However, it seems the committee decided not to reduce $n$, and the actual contest used the original large-$n$ version. On the contest day, I was at an afterparty after participating in some programming contest, probably SCPC hosted by SCSC, and I asked mingyu331 whether my problem had appeared. I remember mingyu331 describing my problem as a strange heuristic problem.

Looking at the discussion thread, I saw that there was a short and elegant solution different from mine: https://codeforces.com/blog/entry/143015. My solution is probably easier to think of but very difficult to implement, so it seems many people solved it using that method. The linked solution is concise in implementation but not easy to come up with, and it is very elegant, so I thought this issue was much less serious than the previous year's.

If $n$ had been a little smaller, I think it could have become a good problem with several difficult solutions. Still, I was relieved that there was no serious problem like the previous year.

APIO 2026: Task B, Navigation

https://qoj.ac/contest/3763/problem/17644

Through two APIO problemsetting experiences, I had come to enjoy submitting problems to international Call for Tasks processes. To get the title of having authored APIO problems for three consecutive years, I made various problems, and this was one decent problem that came out of that process.

The process of making the problem was not very difficult. Recently, I had been trying to create problems by combining random objects, and the elements I decided to combine were "orientations" and "coloring." After realizing that there is not much one can do on a general tree, I added conditions on the degrees of the tree and tried several compromises that would make the problem solve beautifully.

The strongest compromise I found was the version using at most four colors on trees whose degrees are either $1$ or at least $5$. This corresponds to subtasks 3 and 4 of the actual problem. Unfortunately, this was a solution I found while lying down to sleep, and after waking up I somehow mistakenly thought that five colors was the limit. I then wrote the problem and solution for submission under that misconception. While writing the solution, I felt it was a bit easier than I had originally felt, and this was probably the reason.

In any case, I submitted this problem along with several others to APIO 2026 Call for Tasks, and in the end only this problem was selected.

The PDF note included an image of Shinonome Ena simply because there was no other suitable image to include. Shinonome Ena is cute.

After asking dadas08 and checking the actual problem, I saw that the committee had independently reduced the maximum number of colors to four under the degree-at-least-five constraint, and had also added a subtask 5 where more colors are available under slightly smaller degree conditions. I have not thought about that subtask much, but it probably is not too hard; I think I will solve it later.

I expected this problem to be a medium-difficulty problem that was not very hard, so I was shocked to see that nobody solved it. Perhaps it was because many Chinese contestants did not participate in the main contest. I cannot imagine a world where Chinese LGMs fail to get full score on this problem.

This problem also had a solution different from what I had in mind, and I think each solution has its pros and cons. I simply split cases over all possible partitions of $6$ and applied a solution that used as little cleverness as possible.

By setting this problem, I was able to obtain the title of having authored APIO problems for three consecutive years. Since I also think this problem has fairly satisfying quality, I feel satisfied with this problemsetting experience.

Closing

In the past, I solved many problems for various reasons, but recently I have not been solving that many. Partly this is because I have become busier, and partly because there is not much left for me to gain by improving my skills in that direction. These days I tend to solve only problems that look interesting, and spend more thought on making problems. I think I will continue working on problemsetting in order to make more interesting and impressive problems.
